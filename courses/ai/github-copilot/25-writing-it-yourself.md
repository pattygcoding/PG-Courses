## The comparison to make

Reading a suggestion takes a few seconds, and so does typing a short line. The tool pays when the
answer is long and patterned. It costs when the answer is short, novel, or already decided in your
head.

The judgement is not about the tool. It is about the shape of the work in front of you.

## Where typing wins

Five cases come up often enough to name.

- A line you already have in mind. Reading a suggestion you are going to reject is slower than typing
  the four words you were going to write.
- A problem you are working out. While reasoning through an algorithm, a suggestion interrupts the
  reasoning and adds a decision to make.
- Configuration and one-off scripts, where the right answer depends on your environment rather than on
  a pattern.
- Anything you are learning. Accepting code you could not have written removes the exercise, and the
  exercise was the point of being there.
- The line that has to be exactly right, such as a comparison in a pricing rule, where reviewing the
  suggestion costs more than the typing would.

## The cost of a rejection

A rejected suggestion is not free. It is a small decision repeated hundreds of times a day, and the
cost is attention rather than time. That is the strongest argument for switching the feature off in a
file you are thinking hard in.

## A rule that fits most days

A workable default is to leave suggestions on while writing code whose shape you already know, and
switch them off while working something out. The language switch from the second lesson is the nearest
most editors get to that, so in practice it means turning it off for the file where the thinking is
happening.

## Check yourself

For one working day, note every suggestion you reject, grouped by why: you knew the line, you did not
want the interruption, or it was wrong. Then switch suggestions off for the file where most of them
landed and see whether the day goes better.
